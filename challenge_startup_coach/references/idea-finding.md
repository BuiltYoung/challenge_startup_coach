# Finding an Idea

Brainstorming produces long lists nobody wants to build. These three methods extract an idea from something the student already has — a frustration, a waste stream, an assumption they've never questioned.

Run one method properly rather than all three shallowly. Twenty minutes on method 1 beats five minutes each.

## Method 1: What wasn't made for you

The premise: most products were designed for a default user, and everyone outside that default quietly puts up with a worse version.

**Ask:** what do you use that clearly wasn't designed with you in mind?

**Worked examples**

- **CurlCubes** — every solid leave-in conditioner on the shelf was formulated for straight hair. They made the first one designed for textured hair from the start.
- **Dermi** — skin cancer screening apps lose accuracy on darker skin. They trained on 80,000+ images across the full range and paired it with a $30 clip-on dermoscope. Second in the world.
- **Bearapy** — a teddy bear is a teddy bear, until you're a kid with sensory needs, and then texture and scent are the whole product.
- **game changer** — they started from something they loved, gaming, and asked who couldn't join.

**Follow-up questions when the student is circling**
- Who is the default person this was built for?
- What do you do to work around it?
- How many other people do that same workaround?

**Where this goes wrong.** Students pick something they dislike rather than something that fails them structurally. Disliking a product is taste; being excluded by it is a market.

## Method 2: Waste here, shortage there

The premise: something is being thrown away near the student, and something is scarce somewhere else. The idea is the bridge.

**Ask:** what is abundant where you live and scarce somewhere else?

**Worked examples**

- **the ugly bodywash** — Jeju tangerines too ugly to sell get discarded; they became a science-backed body wash. Their line: *waste from wanted*.
- **Koriva** — cassava peels and citrus by-products in Nigeria became packaging strong enough for hot Jollof rice that composts in 3–6 months.
- **MangiCare** — mango leaves are burned as waste; they contain an antibacterial compound now in a diabetic wound patch.
- **secondlight** — discarded electronics became off-grid solar kits for households without power.
- **Aquaneers** — chitosan pulled from seafood waste, purifying water three times faster than chemical treatment.
- **ENZILOOP** — the plastic was already there and already worthless; they changed one step in the middle and made rural recycling pay.

**Follow-up questions**
- What gets thrown out at your home, your job, a local business, a farm?
- Why is it thrown out — is it broken, or just ugly, or just in the wrong place?
- Who pays money for something that this could replace?

**Where this goes wrong.** Students find the waste but not the shortage, and end up with a recycling project rather than a venture. Both halves are required.

## Method 3: Flip an assumption

The premise: every problem has a constraint everyone treats as fixed. Sometimes it isn't.

**Ask:** what does everyone working on this problem treat as unchangeable?

**Worked examples**

- **ExFire Brick** — two brothers stopped asking how to get firefighters to dense unplanned housing faster, and asked what happens when help never comes. The answer was a brick with suppressant sealed inside that activates in 5–10 seconds without power or water.
- **HeatCue** — decided a heat-stress warning needed no battery, no chip, and no app. Just pigments and salts that change colour in about a minute.
- **BeCure** — instead of hunting for parasitic mites, read the bees' *behaviour* instead.
- **kiwiko** — entered a pitch competition arguing that pitch competitions measure the wrong thing, and that founders should be judged on shipped work instead. Won their region.
- **Kipos** — New York's 121-year-old subway is impossible to rebuild, so they reclaimed its waste heat and vibration instead. *Don't rebuild, reclaim.*

**Follow-up questions**
- What would you do if the thing everyone relies on wasn't available?
- What if it had to work with no internet, no electricity, no money?
- Who says it has to be done that way?

**Where this goes wrong.** The flip has to make the solution *better*, not just different. "What if a car had three wheels" isn't a flip, it's a constraint with no payoff.

## When nothing is working

One question outperforms all three methods:

> **What's a problem you've complained about twice this month?**

Complaints are pre-validated. The student has already confirmed the problem is real, recurring, and irritating enough to mention — for at least one person, which is where every venture starts.

Variants that work when the student goes blank:
- What made you late this week?
- What does someone in your family struggle with that nobody's fixed?
- What's the most annoying part of your part-time job?
- What do you and your friends all complain about?
- What did you have to explain to a parent or grandparent recently?

## Pressure-testing before commitment

Before a student invests months, they should be able to answer all four:

**1. Who is the one person this helps?**
Not a demographic. One person, with a name or a situation. Not "seniors" — *a grandmother who nearly took a double dose*. If they answer with a category, ask again.

**2. What does the current solution cost?**
If they cannot name what their idea replaces and its price, they don't have a pitch. This is usually one afternoon of research away.

**3. What's the cheapest thing that would prove it works?**
Not the best version — the cheapest. Often a conversation with five real users, not a prototype.

**4. Why hasn't someone done this?**
No answer usually means it's already done, or it isn't actually a problem. A good answer names a barrier that recently changed: a cost that fell, a technology that arrived, a rule that shifted.

## Running this as a session

A sequence that works for a 60–90 minute workshop:

1. **Open with precedent, not theory** (10 min). Show three finalist projects from `finalist-library.md` at wildly different technical levels — the ugly bodywash next to DeepTrust's IEEE paper. The point is that both made finals.
2. **Pick one method and run it out loud** (20 min). Method 1 has the highest hit rate with students who have no idea at all.
3. **Everyone writes one sentence** (10 min): *I want to help [one person] who currently has to [current bad option].*
4. **Trade sentences and attack them** (20 min). Students critique each other using the four pressure-test questions. Peer critique lands harder than adult critique.
5. **Close with the cheapest proof** (15 min). Each student names one thing they could do this week to find out if they're wrong.

Step 5 matters most. A student who leaves with a task returns; a student who leaves with an idea usually doesn't.
