# Winning Patterns

Six patterns that separate finalists from the rest, each with the evidence behind it. Use these to evaluate an idea, and use the named examples to make a critique concrete rather than abstract.

A critique lands when it comes with a comparable. "Your accuracy claim has no test behind it" is forgettable. "BeeGuard validated across four real colonies before submitting — what's your version of that?" gives the student something to copy.

## 1. AI is table stakes; evidence isn't

Nearly every finalist uses AI somewhere. It is no longer a differentiator. What distinguishes the teams that advance is AI aimed at one specific problem, with something behind it proving it works.

- **DeepTrust** — 95%+ accuracy on multi-modal deepfake detection, with **peer-reviewed IEEE publications** behind it. High schoolers published in a real journal.
- **BeeGuard** — 94.7% accuracy diagnosing hive health, sub-second, zero hardware cost, **validated across four real colonies with 100% intervention success**.
- **BeCure** — rather than hunting mites directly, trained AI to read bee *behaviour*, then **field-tested with the Korean Beekeepers Association** and made it run offline on a basic phone.
- **Dermi** — most skin cancer apps lose accuracy on darker skin; trained on **80,000+ images across the full range**, 95%+ in under 10 seconds.

The test: can the student name what they did to find out whether it works? A demo is not evidence. A number from a test is.

Note the asymmetry — Conrad demands this, Diamond tolerates its absence. Check the target competition before insisting.

## 2. The winning sentence is a cost comparison

The strongest pitches almost all contain a version of: *the existing solution costs $X, ours costs $Y.*

- **Echo/Pulse** — $2,500, against $10,500 AAC tablets and $50,000–$100,000 brain implants
- **Clarity** — under $250, against $10,000+ laboratory equipment
- **Vigil** — under $100 per hospital bed, replacing 4–6 hour manual spot checks
- **SeedShift** — $499 with 1–2 season payback, for farmers who could never afford industrial equipment
- **Dermi** — a $30 clip-on dermoscope instead of a dermatologist waitlist
- **AlziDetect** — a free browser-and-webcam gaze test instead of an MRI as first screen
- **Positive Impact Maker** — a CPR aid kit at about 1.5% the price of an AED

If a student can't name what their idea replaces and what that costs today, they don't have a pitch yet. This is often the fastest single fix available — an afternoon of research turns a vague idea into a sentence a judge remembers.

## 3. The business case reads like an investment

Conrad finalists submit full market sizing, and the numbers are specific rather than gestural.

- **AeroLattice** — $250,000 of value per aircraft, across **18,000+ specific 737/A320 airframes**, in a $1.8B market
- **Aphelion** — ~$60M modelled annual licensing revenue; 20% lighter, 61.8% smaller, 71% less power
- **Kinection** — profitable by year 3, $26M+ net profit by year 10
- **OctoScope** — $14M projected net income and 61% ROI by 2035

Note what makes these credible: they're derived, not asserted. "18,000+ 737/A320 airframes" is countable. A DECA national champion derived customer acquisition cost to the cent — $370,000 ÷ 90,000 users = $4.11.

Teams that treat the business plan as the thing you write at the end don't advance.

## 4. Claim a small share of a big market

Counterintuitive and consistent. **Fly-By**, a DECA national champion, won while targeting **less than 1%** of its market — 90,000 users out of 196 million travelers.

The instinct is to project impressive share to look ambitious. Judges read that as naive. A small share of a large market reads as achievable, and achievable is what gets funded.

Related: **CeliusVorox** priced its spacecraft skin at $128,500/m² but framed it as **under 1% of mission cost**. Same technique — make the number feel small relative to something the buyer already spends.

## 5. Name one person, not a cause

Every social-track finalist solves a tightly defined problem for a clearly identifiable person.

Echo/Pulse opens its brief like this:

> In a quiet room in Naperville, Mr. Zhou, an 82-year-old stroke survivor, cries because he cannot say "I love you" to his grandson.

One person. One room. One sentence he can't say.

Compare the specificity of the finalists against the generic version:

| Generic | What actually placed |
|---|---|
| Improving healthcare | **defyai** — overturning a specific insurance denial |
| Supporting seniors | **medlas ai** — catching a dosage error before it's swallowed |
| Helping people with disabilities | **game changer** — a controller for players with limited dexterity |
| Environmental sustainability | **Koriva** — packaging that holds 80°C Jollof rice and composts in 3–6 months |
| Prison reform | **bridge** — income, skills, and a structured path back for people leaving prison |

Broad mission statements do not place. Ask the student who the one person is; if they answer with a demographic, push again.

## 6. Specificity beats scope

Software winners are consistently "a tool for one group," never a general platform.

- **AuthAI** — claim intelligence for physicians, not "healthcare AI"
- **Harmoniq** — music therapy for dementia patients, with insights routed to the caregiver
- **Lumera AI** — adaptive learning for Nigerian classrooms, closing gaps rather than accelerating students already ahead
- **PocketPhysio** — 17 joints tracked 30 times a second, reports auto-sent to the patient's own therapist

Students reach for scope because it sounds ambitious. It reads as unfocused. The narrower the user, the more credible the solution.

## Scoring an idea

Run these six checks. Three or fewer clear passes means the idea needs work before submission, not polish.

| # | Check | Passing looks like |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Evidence | A number from an actual test, not a demo |
| 2 | Cost comparison | Can name the incumbent and its price |
| 3 | Business case | Market sized from countable units |
| 4 | Market share | Targets a small slice, and says so |
| 5 | One person | Names a situation, not a demographic |
| 6 | Specificity | One user group, clearly bounded |

Weight by competition. Conrad: 1 and 3 are decisive. Diamond: 5 and storytelling carry a quarter of the score. DECA: follow the published rubric section by section. Blue Ocean: 2 and 5, and the market must be genuinely unserved.

## Common failure modes

**"It's an AI platform for X."** Platform means the student hasn't picked a user. Ask who the first hundred users are, by name or description.

**No number anywhere.** The most common gap, and the most fixable. One afternoon of research usually produces a defensible cost comparison.

**The market size is a headline figure.** "$4.5 billion market" copied from an article isn't sizing. Sizing is: how many units, at what price, and why can we reach that many.

**Solving a problem the student doesn't have and doesn't know anyone who has.** These ideas die in Q&A. Soaring Seeds spent **four years** in field work with nomadic herding households before building; that's the extreme end, but proximity to the problem shows.

**Two ideas fused together.** Usually a sign the student is attached to a technology rather than a problem. Ask which half they'd keep.

**The idea is already a product.** Worth checking early — but note that "already done" is often wrong. CurlCubes entered a shelf full of conditioners. Dermi entered a field full of screening apps. Both won by finding who the existing versions failed.
